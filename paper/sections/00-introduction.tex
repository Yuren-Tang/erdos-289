\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

We use $\N=\Z_{\ge0}$ and $\N_{>0}=\{1,2,\ldots\}$.  Let $\mathcal C$ denote the family of finite subsets $S\subset\N_{>0}$ whose connected components in the path graph on $\N_{>0}$ have cardinality $2$ or $3$.  Write
\[
\nu(S)=|\pi_0(S)|,
\qquad
W(S)=\sum_{n\in S}\frac1n.
\]

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every sufficiently large integer $k$ there exists $S\in\mathcal C$ such that
\[
\nu(S)=k,
\qquad
W(S)=1.
\]
\end{theorem}

Thus every sufficiently large $k$ is realized by exactly $k$ pairwise disjoint, nonadjacent integer intervals, all of length $2$ or $3$.  This proves the strengthened formulation currently listed as Erd\H{o}s Problem 289 \cite{ErdosProblem289}.  Erd\H{o}s and Graham posed the underlying interval-reciprocal problem in \cite[p.~34]{ErdosGraham1980}; their printed formulation does not explicitly impose distinctness, disjointness, or nonadjacency.  The current problem list records the strengthened version used here \cite{ErdosProblem289}.

The final reduction is elementary.  A nonempty configuration with
\[
0<W(S)<2,
\qquad
W(S)\equiv0\pmod{\Z}
\]
must have $W(S)=1$.  We therefore work modulo $1$ through most of the construction and retain a fixed margin below reciprocal mass $2$.

The proof is driven by three constructions.  First, at each sufficiently large prime-power stage $Q=p^e$, a signed-inverse construction produces $\gg Q/\log Q$ short intervals whose residues in the new simple quotient of $\Q/\Z$ are controlled, while their mutual incompatibility and total reciprocal cost remain bounded.  Second, a reciprocal-neutral identity replaces two components by three without changing reciprocal value.  Remote copies of this identity form Boolean families that widen an interval of possible component counts at arbitrarily small reciprocal cost.  Third, restricted sumset estimates convert every sufficiently dense part of a signed-inverse family into a uniform transfer across the corresponding simple quotient.

These local transfers reduce the middle of the proof to one scalar variable: the width of the available interval of component counts.  At prime stages the width gains order $q/\log q$; the total loss contributed by composite prime powers up to $X$ is $o(X/\log X)$.  A bounded-lag earlier prime therefore pays for every sufficiently late step, and the successive count intervals cover a final ray.

It remains to turn this residue-and-count calculation into one literal configuration of pairwise compatible intervals.  This is postponed until a finite terminal cutoff $X$ has been chosen.  The relevant interval families then form a finite bounded-degree incompatibility graph, and Haxell's independent-transversal theorem supplies dense compatible subfamilies at all stages through $X$.  The local transfer theorem was proved for every sufficiently dense subfamily, so the same scalar calculation survives this final thinning.

The categorical language below isolates the compositional issue in this last passage.  Observable residue-and-count relations may compose even when the concrete interval labels witnessing their edges cannot coexist.  Compatible configurations form a partial commutative monoid, and
\[
\Omega=(\overline W,\nu):\mathcal C\longrightarrow(\Q/\Z)\times\N
\]
is additive wherever the partial sum is defined.  We package labelled observable relations into a locally posetal $2$-category $\mathbf{Rel}_{\mathcal C}^{\Omega}$.  Forgetting labels gives a normal oplax $2$-functor
\[
U:\mathbf{Rel}_{\mathcal C}^{\Omega}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Rel};
\]
its comparison $2$-cell records precisely the possible loss of witnesses caused by incompatible labels.  Once Haxell's finite selection supplies joint compatibility, the comparison becomes an identity, so the local transfers compose to a genuine interval configuration.  The prime-power filtration and the passage from simple quotients to affine torsor fibres are expressed by the corresponding quotient and pullback diagrams.

At the terminal state we obtain residue $0$, component count $k$, and reciprocal mass below $2$.  The commutative square
\[
\begin{CD}
\mathcal C @>{W}>> \Q\\
@V{\overline W}VV @VV{q_{\Z}}V\\
\Q/\Z @= \Q/\Z
\end{CD}
\]
then shows that the positive reciprocal value is an integer strictly below $2$, and hence equals $1$.
